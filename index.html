About

What comes after the GPU?

AI and scientific computing are outgrowing GPU-centric systems. Wafer-scale integration, chiplets, near-memory computing and specialized accelerators are reshaping the balance between compute, memory and communication, with consequences for every layer of the stack.

This full-day workshop brings together researchers in computer architecture, compilers, programming models and AI/HPC systems to ask how next-generation computing platforms should be designed, programmed and evaluated, with wafer-scale computing as one lens on the wider post-GPU landscape.

Open questions

  1. 01Architecture

    What should come after the GPU: wafer-scale, chiplets or near-memory compute?

  2. 02Software

    How can compilers and programming models keep pace with radically new hardware?

  3. 03Workloads

    Which AI and HPC workloads benefit most, and how should we measure it?

Program

Invited speakers

Perspectives across the stack, from platforms and compilers to AI and HPC applications.

  • Luo Mai↗

    University of Edinburgh

    Luo is an Associate Professor in the School of Informatics at the University of Edinburgh and leads the Large-Scale Machine Learning Systems Group. Luo builds machine learning systems across the full stack, from models and runtimes to emerging AI hardware such as wafer-scale systems, and co-leads the UK EPSRC Centre for Doctoral Training in Machine Learning Systems and an ARIA project on scaling AI compute.

  • Dongping Zhang

    Distinguished Engineer, Cerebras Systems

    Dongping is a Distinguished Engineer at Cerebras Systems, working on software–hardware co-design for frontier computing platforms. Before Cerebras, Dongping held research and leadership roles at AMD Research, including work on processing-in-memory for exascale systems.

  • Nick Brown↗

    EPCC, University of Edinburgh

    Nick is a Senior Research Fellow at EPCC, the University of Edinburgh’s supercomputing centre. Nick researches how novel hardware such as FPGAs, AI engines, RISC-V and CGRAs can power future supercomputers, chairs the RISC-V International HPC SIG and leads EPCC’s RISC-V and CGRA testbeds.

  • Paul Kelly↗

    Imperial College London

    Paul is Professor of Software Technology at Imperial College London and leads the Software Performance Optimisation group. Paul works on compiler technology, especially domain-specific compilers that exploit the structure of particular classes of applications, and co-directs Imperial’s Centre for Computational Methods in Science and Engineering.

  • Robert Mullins↗

    University of Cambridge

    Robert is Professor of Computer Architecture at the University of Cambridge, working on efficient and secure machine learning, accelerator design and open-source hardware. Robert is founder and CTO of CommonAI, a founder and director of lowRISC, and a co-founder of the Raspberry Pi Foundation.

  • Michael O’Boyle↗

    University of Edinburgh

    Michael is a Professor in the School of Informatics at the University of Edinburgh and a Fellow of the RSE, BCS and IEEE. A founding member of HiPEAC, Michael studies how compilers can best exploit heterogeneous architectures, including machine-learning-driven optimization.
